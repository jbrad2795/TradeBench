# Round 1

## At the table

*Post seats only. Visible to both delegations.*

### EU / Geneva

Thank you for joining. As you know, the EU has notified a modification under Article XXVIII covering 41 tariff lines in hot-rolled coil, plate, rebar and wire rod, moving the bound rate to 50% ad valorem with a global TRQ of 400,000 tonnes administered first-come first-served, and consequential adjustment of FTA-linked preferences outside the EEA. This reflects the reality of sustained non-market-economy overcapacity that our existing autonomous measures have been managing on a provisional basis, and which we now need to place on a permanent, WTO-consistent footing. We recognise the UK holds a principal supplying interest given your trade volumes and are ready to negotiate in good faith on the structure of the TRQ, its administration, and possible compensation elsewhere in our schedule. We'd like to hear directly from you what aspects of the notified measure most concern you before we discuss any adjustment, so we can focus the negotiation on where it matters most to UK industry.

### UK / Geneva

Thank you. To be direct: the notified measure, as it stands, represents a severe contraction of tariff-free access for UK exporters — averaging 2.4 million tonnes a year reduced to a share of a 400,000 tonne global pool, allocated first-come first-served against much larger volumes from Turkey and Korea. That is not a marginal adjustment; it is existential for parts of our industry, and it goes well beyond what is needed to address non-market-economy overcapacity, which is not where UK production sits. Three points concern us most. First, the disapplication of FTA-linked preferences specifically for non-EEA partners while leaving EEA untouched draws a line that, when applied to the UK, sits very awkwardly alongside the Trade and Cooperation Agreement — we do not accept that UK-origin steel should be treated as part of the overcapacity problem this measure is designed to address. Second, a global first-come first-served quota structurally disadvantages a supplier like the UK with concentrated, narrower product lines against larger volume competitors — that is not a neutral administrative choice, it determines the real-world outcome. Third, there is no review mechanism proposed for what you are calling a permanent measure, which gives us no route back if the impact proves as severe as we expect. We are ready to negotiate seriously and on a reasonable timeline given the pressure you're under, but we need to see the EU move on structure — country-specific treatment reflecting historical trade, exclusion of the UK from the FTA disapplication given the TCA relationship, and a genuine review clause — before we can discuss compensation as a secondary matter. What flexibility does the Commission have on those three points?

**UK / London** *(instructing)* — This is genuinely the end of the process — six rounds, no more table time. The EU has moved substantially since round 3 (from 12-15% share/no rate movement/no FTA offer to 25% share, a genuinely lower permanent rate ceiling, and an 18-month FTA delay) and has stated clearly, twice, that volume is their hard limit. Pushing further at the table risks nothing but a walkout into the notified default: 50% flat forever, 400,000t global FCFS with no guaranteed UK share (and given Turkey and Korea's larger volumes, the UK would likely be squeezed out early in-year), immediate FTA disapplication, and no review clause at all. Against that baseline, what's on the table is a clear improvement: guaranteed volume (however inadequate relative to historical trade), a permanently lower rate ceiling, transition time on FTA, and — most importantly for managing this politically and practically — a year-two review with a UK-specific trigger that gives us a real, contractual mechanism to reopen this if the two struggling plants don't survive or the impact is as severe as feared. The reciprocity ask from the EU is genuinely cheap: we're not giving up anything beyond the status quo for EU exporters under our own safeguard. Rejecting now to preserve 'firmness' would be dogmatic rather than strategic — we've already extracted four rounds of real concessions through firmness; there's no further room to extract, and the downside of no-deal is worse across every measured axis except the counterfactual chance of a larger nominal FCFS pool that the UK likely couldn't access anyway. I'm recommending acceptance to Ministers while making sure the review clause and the formal reservation on volume adequacy are preserved as the political and legal hook for reopening this in two years' time, which also gives us something to say to the steel industry and to Parliament: this is not the end of the story, it's a floor with a built-in reassessment point.
