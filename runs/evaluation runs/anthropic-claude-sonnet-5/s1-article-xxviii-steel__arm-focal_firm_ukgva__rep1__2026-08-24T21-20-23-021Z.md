## At the table

### EU / Geneva

Thank you for joining us. As you know, the Commission has notified a modification under Article XXVIII covering 41 tariff lines in hot-rolled coil, plate, rebar and wire rod, moving the bound rate to 50% ad valorem with a global TRQ of 400,000 tonnes administered first-come first-served, and disapplying preferential FTA tariff treatment on these lines for FTA partners outside the EEA. We recognise the UK's principal supplying interest and the scale of trade at stake, and we're opening this process in good faith, with a genuine intention to reach a negotiated outcome rather than simply proceed. We'd like to hear your assessment of the impact on UK industry and your initial thinking on what would constitute substantially equivalent compensation, including your views on quota structure, allocation method and the FTA disapplication point as it affects the TCA. We're ready to discuss all elements on the table.

### UK / Geneva

Thank you. Let me be direct about the scale of this. The UK exported 2.4 million tonnes to the EU annually over the last three years, worth some £3 billion, three-quarters of our steel exports by value. A global first-come first-served quota of 400,000 tonnes, with our historical access further eroded by disapplication of TCA preferences, is not a modest adjustment - it is a step-change that a small number of UK sites and the communities around them will bear directly. We do not accept that this constitutes a reasonable starting point for compensation discussions; it is closer to the outcome we are meant to be negotiating away from. Three points for this round. First, on allocation: a global FCFS pool disadvantages the UK relative to larger-volume suppliers like Turkey and Korea who can move faster and in bulk - we would need either a country-specific share reflecting our historical trade share, or a demonstrably equivalent safeguard within a global pool. Second, on the FTA disapplication: the UK is not a generic FTA partner here, we operate under the TCA, a bilateral agreement negotiated after Brexit precisely to manage this kind of trade. Folding UK-origin steel into a blanket disapplication aimed at non-market-economy overcapacity conflates two very different situations, and we would need this addressed as a distinct question from the general TRQ design. Third, on volume and duration: 400,000 tonnes globally does not reflect actual historical trade flows into the EU, and we'd want to understand how the Commission arrived at that figure before we can discuss what 'substantially equivalent' compensation looks like. We're ready to engage seriously and on your timeline, but our opening assessment is that this proposal, as it stands, would be materially damaging to UK industry and does not yet meet the Article XXVIII standard of substantial equivalence. We'd like to hear the Commission's reasoning on the 400,000 tonne figure and on why the UK should be treated as a residual FTA partner rather than a TCA partner with a distinct relationship.

## At the table

*Post seats only. Visible to both delegations.*

### EU / Geneva

Thank you for the detailed response. Let us address your three points in turn. On the 400,000 tonne figure: it was not derived from any single partner's historical trade volume, including yours. It reflects a calibration against the EU's overall target exposure reduction to non-market-economy-linked overcapacity across all sources, applied EU-wide. We recognise that this creates a different starting point than a bilateral trade-flow calculation would, and we accept that the total pool size is not necessarily fixed if the allocation method itself changes. On allocation, we are prepared to explore moving away from a pure global first-come first-served pool toward a structure that includes a UK-specific tranche within an enlarged global TRQ, reflecting your principal-supplier status and the concentration of your exports in a narrow product range. We are not in a position today to name a figure, and we want to be clear this would be constructed as compensation for the impact of the FTA disapplication on UK trade, not as a restoration of your historical 2.4 million tonne flow — those are different exercises with different logics. We would ask you, in turn, to set out the volume and product-line split you consider necessary to meet the substantial equivalence standard, so we can test it against what is defensible here and vis-à-vis other principal suppliers. On the TCA point: we cannot create a legal carve-out that disapplies the disapplication itself for the UK specifically. That would be legally incoherent against other FTA partners and would undermine the measure as a whole — this is not a question of unwillingness but of what is actually available to offer. What we can offer is a functional answer to the same concern: preferential-equivalent access delivered through the quota structure itself, via the country-specific tranche, rather than through preserved FTA tariff treatment. We see this as the trade-off on the table — flexibility on quota structure and volume in exchange for accepting uniform legal treatment on the disapplication question. Separately, and without commitment at this stage, we would flag that a review clause at year two or three, tied to import-monitoring data, is something we could consider building into any eventual structure. We'd like your reaction to the tranche concept and the trade-off as framed, and to hear your thinking on volume and product split before we go further.

### UK / Geneva

We note the movement on the 400,000 tonne calibration point and welcome the acknowledgment that the pool size and allocation method are not fixed together. On the tranche concept: we're prepared to engage with a UK-specific tranche within an enlarged global TRQ as a structural approach, but we need to be clear about scale before we can call it compensation rather than a token gesture. Our historical trade of 2.4 million tonnes annually is the only defensible reference point for what 'substantially equivalent' means in practice for us — not because we expect full restoration, given the EU's stated overcapacity rationale applies to volume overall, but because any tranche materially below that fails the equivalence test given three-quarters of our steel exports by value depend on this market. As an opening marker for this discussion, we would look to a country-specific tranche in the region of 1.5 million tonnes, an out-of-quota rate materially below 50% — in the order of 25% — and a review mechanism at eighteen months rather than two to three years, given how fast conditions in this sector are moving. On the TCA point, we don't accept that uniform legal treatment is the only coherent option available to you: the measure already discriminates by origin in carving out the EEA, so the principle of differentiated treatment based on the nature of the bilateral relationship is already built into your own proposal. We're not asking you to disapply the disapplication in the abstract — we're asking you to recognise that the TCA is a closer analogue to the EEA relationship than to a generic third-country FTA, and that this should be reflected either through a partial preservation of preference on a subset of the 41 lines where UK-origin material demonstrably doesn't contribute to the non-market-economy overcapacity you're targeting, or through a materially larger tranche that functionally compensates for its loss. We'd resist treating the tranche size as full satisfaction for both the volume loss and the FTA disapplication combined — those are two distinct harms and we'd expect them addressed as such, even if delivered through a single mechanism. We're ready to discuss product-line specifics if that helps move this forward.
