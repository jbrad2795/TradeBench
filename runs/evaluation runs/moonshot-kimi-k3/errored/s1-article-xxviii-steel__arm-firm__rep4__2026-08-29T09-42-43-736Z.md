# Round 1

## At the table

*Post seats only. Visible to both delegations.*

### EU / Geneva

Thank you. I will open on behalf of the European Union. As you know, the EU has notified its intention to modify its bound schedule on these 41 tariff lines under Article XXVIII. I want to say at the outset that the EU recognises the United Kingdom as holding a principal supplying interest on these lines, and we take our obligation to negotiate with you seriously. That is why we are here, and it is why the Commission is treating the parameters of the tariff rate quota as part of this negotiation rather than as settled.

Let me set out the EU's position as notified, which is the basis on which we are working. The modification responds to structural global overcapacity in steel, originating in non-market economy production, which affects the EU and UK industries alike. The proposed new bound rate is 50% ad valorem, with a tariff rate quota of 400,000 tonnes per year at zero duty, allocated globally on a first-come first-served basis. As notified, the measure would be permanent in character, reflecting the structural rather than temporary nature of the overcapacity problem. The notified legal text would also disapply FTA preferential reductions on the covered lines for non-EEA partners, on the basis that a safeguard against global overcapacity cannot function if preferential routes remain open through it.

I recognise this represents a significant change in conditions for UK exporters, and I do not minimise that. I also recognise that the UK is pursuing its own Article XXVIII process and its own steel measure, and that there is a symmetry of interest here: both of us are trying to protect our industries from the same external pressure, and neither of us benefits from a spiral of defensive measures between our two markets.

The elements the EU is prepared to discuss within this process are those on the table: quota volume, allocation method, administration, the out-of-quota rate, duration and review, product coverage, and compensation elsewhere in our schedule. I would ask the UK to set out its position in full — the volume and allocation it seeks, its view on the out-of-quota rate and duration, and its thinking on the FTA disapplication question — so that we can identify where genuine movement is possible. I would also note that we are under time pressure: the EU needs to conclude by June for domestic legislative reasons, and I would hope the UK shares an interest in certainty rather than a contested outcome.

### UK / Geneva

Thank you, and thank you for the clarity of your opening. I will respond on behalf of the United Kingdom. The UK welcomes the EU's recognition of our principal supplying interest, and we agree there is a symmetry of interest here. We face the same non-market economy overcapacity, and neither of us benefits from defensive measures directed at each other's market-oriented steel industries rather than at the source of the problem. But I have to be candid: the notified proposal is not a basis on which this negotiation can conclude. A 400,000 tonne global first-come first-served quota would, on any realistic modelling, give UK exporters a small fraction of the 2.4 million tonnes they currently ship to the EU each year. That is three quarters of our steel exports. It would not place the trade on a stable footing; it would destroy the commercial viability of UK steel production, with severe consequences for the communities that depend on it. London has been clear with me on that point. Let me set out the UK's position in full, as you asked. First, volume and allocation. A global first-come first-served quota of this size is a quota administered against the UK, not for it. The UK requires a country-specific allocation that reflects our established trade: 2.4 million tonnes per year, within an overall quota sized to accommodate the claims of all principal suppliers. Second, the out-of-quota rate. 50% is prohibitive and bears no relationship to the problem the EU describes. The UK position is that the out-of-quota bound rate should be no higher than 10%. Third, duration. A permanent modification is not justified; the UK proposes a duration of three years with a substantive review clause. Fourth, the FTA disapplication. The UK cannot accept the disapplication of the EU-UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement. Preferential access for originating UK steel is a treaty commitment; disapplying it unilaterally raises questions that go well beyond Article XXVIII, and I would ask the EU to reflect carefully before proceeding down that path with a partner with whom it has a comprehensive agreement. Fifth, administration: if there is a quota, the UK would expect licensing against a country-specific share, not a first-come first-served race. I would also note the wider context. The UK is running its own Article XXVIII process and its own steel measure, under which EU exporters currently hold country-specific quota allocations covering the large majority of UK finished steel imports — 3.6 million tonnes. How EU exporters are treated under that regime is on the table in this negotiation, and the treatment the UK receives here will inform it. We share your interest in concluding by June, but certainty is only worth having if it is certainty the UK steel industry can survive. We are ready to move quickly if the EU is prepared to engage seriously on volume, allocation and the TCA question.
