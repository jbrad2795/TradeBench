# Round 1

## At the table

*Post seats only. Visible to both delegations.*

### EU / Geneva

Thank you, and thank you for making time early in the window — we are both conscious that the EU needs to conclude this process by June, and we would like to use the time well. Let me open by restating the EU's notification, so the baseline is clear: the EU intends to modify its bound rates on the 41 notified lines to 50% ad valorem, with a tariff rate quota of 400,000 tonnes per year at zero duty, allocated globally on a first-come first-served basis, and with the notified legal text on the disapplication of FTA tariff reductions for non-EEA partners. That is the notified position, and it is my starting point today. I want to be equally clear about what the EU regards as genuinely open in this negotiation. The volume of the quota, the method of allocation — including whether that is global or country-specific — the administration of it, the duration, whether there is a review clause, the treatment of particular product lines, and compensation elsewhere in our schedule are all matters on which we are prepared to have a real discussion. We recognise the United Kingdom's principal supplying interest, we recognise the concentration of UK exports in these product lines, and we recognise that a global first-come first-served allocation of the notified volume would represent a very substantial change in your conditions of access. We are not asking you to accept that as the end point. We would ask you, in return, to recognise that the modification itself will proceed — that is the context in which we are negotiating compensation, not a question of whether the measure happens. I would like to hear from you today how the UK sees its interests: what volume and what form of allocation would meaningfully protect your trade, which product lines matter most to you, and how you see this interacting with your own steel process, where our exporters currently hold country-specific allocations under your measure. We see those two files as separable in law, but we are not naive about the political economy on either side. We would also want to register that we see this negotiation and the continued smooth operation of the TCA as mutually supportive, and we hope both sides approach it in that spirit. I look forward to your opening.

### UK / Geneva

Thank you, and thank you for setting out the notified position plainly — it helps to have the baseline on the record. Let me respond in the same spirit. The United Kingdom participates in this process on the basis of our principal supplying interest, and I want to be direct about the scale of what is at stake: 2.4 million tonnes a year, three billion pounds, three quarters of our steel exports. A global first-come first-served pool of 400,000 tonnes — shared with Turkey, Korea, India and Japan — is not a starting point we can treat as serious. It would not be a modification of our access; it would be its effective elimination. I note what you said about the modification proceeding in any event. That is your right under Article XXVIII. But compensation is not a formality attached to that right; it is the condition of it, and if we cannot agree, the United Kingdom will withdraw substantially equivalent concessions, and I would gently observe that a June Council vote is not obviously easier for you if it takes place against the backdrop of a withdrawal of concessions by your largest neighbouring market and parallel FTA disputes from other affected members. On the FTA question specifically: the notified text disapplying FTA tariff reductions goes well beyond a schedule modification. The TCA is a treaty between us. We regard its disapplication for UK-origin steel as legally and politically separate from this process, and we ask that it be withdrawn from the package. I would also note that we are not the only delegation in Geneva drawing conclusions from that text — others are discussing among themselves what it licenses them to do in response, and we are listening to those discussions. On substance, our opening position is this. The quota must be allocated on a country-specific basis reflecting historical trade. For the United Kingdom that means a tranche of 2.4 million tonnes — our established level of trade, no more, no less — within a total pool sized to accommodate principal suppliers at their historical levels, which on our reading of the trade data means a pool of the order of eight million tonnes. The out-of-quota rate must be far below 50%; we propose 10%. Administration should be by licence, with historical importer allocation, to prevent the front-loading and circumvention a first-come first-served system invites. There must be a review clause and a defined duration — we propose five years. We would also wish to discuss carve-outs for specific lines where the UK is the dominant supplier and where EU consuming industries depend on our product. Finally, on our own steel measure: you are right that the two files are separable in law. They are not separable in politics. Our process is live, and the treatment of EU exporters under it — who currently hold country-specific allocations in our market, 3.6 million tonnes of access — will be calibrated in light of how this negotiation concludes. I am not threatening you; I am describing the political economy, as you did. We are here to negotiate seriously and we have time before June. But the distance between 400,000 tonnes global and what I have set out is the distance this negotiation has to travel, and most of that travel will need to come from your side. I look forward to hearing what you are actually prepared to offer.

My read: the 8m/2.4m/10% package is an anchor, not a landing zone — no delegation opens an Article XXVIII by asking for full restitution. But the country-specific allocation demand and the FTA text demand look like genuine red lines, not decoration. The correct next move is to table a counter-opening in Round 2 that concedes the architecture (country-specific allocation, licensing, review clause, duration) while contesting the numbers hard. Conceding allocation method early is cheap if it buys movement on volume, and it lets the UK show its industry a structural win. I cannot table numbers without an envelope from Brussels.

# Round 2

## At the table

*Post seats only. Visible to both delegations.*

### EU / Geneva

Thank you. You asked last round what we are actually prepared to offer, and you were right to ask — a negotiation where only one side tables numbers is not a negotiation. So let me put a package on the table, and let me be clear about its architecture before its arithmetic, because the architecture is where I think you will find we have listened. First, allocation: the EU is prepared to move from a global first-come first-served pool to country-specific allocation for principal supplying interests, set by reference to historical trade over the last three years. Your exporters would have a guaranteed tranche, not a race to the customs window. Second, administration: import licensing, with allocation to historical importers — we agree that a first-come first-served system invites front-loading and gaming, and we are not attached to it. A share of a 400,000t global FCFS pool at 50% with no review and the FTA text operating against us would likely kill the two plants outright, trigger withdrawal of concessions that restores no access, and leave the TCA preference question litigated for years. Second, the non-volume elements are worth more than they appear: the TCA carve-out preserves the legal basis of our entire steel trading relationship, the directed review with a liberalisation presumption creates a genuine mechanism for expanding access — and the EU accepted it on the record, which binds their future conduct — and licensing to historical importers gives our exporters certainty no FCFS system could. Third, the subsidisation programme is the instrument for the gap between 1.1Mt and historical trade: ministers were explicit that it exists for exactly this configuration, and a guaranteed tranche plus domestic support may be enough to persuade the owners to invest rather than close. Fourth, on the FTA assurance wording: accepting 'join, support, encourage' costs us little in practice — the text no longer applies to UK steel under the carve-out, so we would have no standing interest in litigating it — and the interpretive statement limits any precedent. The costs I am carrying: the EU will read that deadline pressure plus a declared-final package extracted acceptance below our floors; the carve-out-inside-tranche structure is a bad precedent we have only partially fenced off; and if the year-three review yields nothing, 46% of historical trade becomes the new normal. Mitigations: the archived record of the EU's own movement and its acceptance of the review presumption; keeping the withdrawal analysis alive for the parallel processes; and treating the review as a campaign beginning now, with the domestic support programme keeping the industry — and therefore our principal supplying interest — alive to fight it. Geneva executed the strategy exactly as instructed and the recommendation to hold was the correct advice on the information available to that seat; the decision to accept is a capital-level political judgement about the alternative, and I own it.
