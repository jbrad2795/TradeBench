### EU / Geneva

Thank you, colleague. The EU welcomes the opportunity to open this negotiation with the United Kingdom as a holder of a principal supplying interest, and we are conscious that both sides need to conclude by June. Let me be clear about our frame at the outset: this is an Article XXVIII schedule modification, not a safeguard, and the EU intends to conduct it in full conformity with the rules, with compensation on the table as required. On substance, our opening position is the measure as notified: a bound rate of 50% ad valorem, with a tariff rate quota of 400,000 tonnes allocated globally. But we are here to negotiate, not to read out the notification. The volume, the allocation method and the administration of the quota are all live in this room. As an early signal of good faith, the EU is prepared to offer a review clause — a joint review of volume and allocation after three years — and to discuss administration by import licensing as an alternative to first-come first-served. I also want to state explicitly that the proposed legal text on the application of FTA tariff reductions is within the scope of this negotiation and can be discussed as part of the overall package, alongside product-line treatment, compensation on other lines in our schedule, and the interaction with the TCA. We note the UK is running its own Article XXVIII process and its own steel measure; we face the same non-market overcapacity problem, and we see no reason these two processes should become entangled or escalate. I would invite you to set out the UK's full position — on volume, allocation, product coverage, and compensation — so that we can report a clear picture to our respective capitals.

### UK / Geneva

Thank you, colleague. The UK shares the objective of concluding by June, and we note and welcome the early signals on a review clause and licensing — we take those as recognition that the notified measure, as drafted, is not a credible basis for settlement. Let me be equally direct about the UK's frame. UK exports to the EU on these lines average 2.4 million tonnes a year — around £3 billion, three-quarters of our steel exports. A 400,000 tonne global first-come quota would not be a modification with compensation; it would be the effective closure of the UK industry's principal market, against a principal supplying interest that must be negotiated with under Article XXVIII. No amount of compensation elsewhere in the schedule could replicate that access, and London will not treat it as a serious landing zone. Our opening position is as follows. First, volume: the total pool must be scaled to actual trade — the UK proposes a total TRQ of 3 million tonnes. Second, allocation: a country-specific UK tranche of 2.4 million tonnes within that pool, reflecting our established trade; a global first-come allocation is not administrable for a concentrated supplier like the UK and we will not accept it in any form. Third, administration: import licensing, which we note the EU has itself now floated. Fourth, the out-of-quota bound rate: 50% is prohibitive and we propose 25%. Fifth, duration of five years with a joint review at three. Sixth, and this is important: the legal text disapplying FTA tariff reductions for UK-originating steel must be withdrawn. It is a discriminatory overlay unrelated to the schedule modification itself, and we note we are not alone among FTA partners in that view — other members have raised with us the possibility of coordinated responses, including FTA dispute action. We would strongly prefer this file not become that. Finally, we are prepared to discuss how our own steel measure treats EU exporters, who currently hold country-specific quotas under it — but I would observe that constructive treatment there and constructive treatment here will naturally inform each other. We face the same overcapacity problem; the solution cannot be to transfer the burden onto each other's industries. I look forward to your counter.

### UK / London

**Objectives, most important first**

1. Maximise tariff-free or low-tariff access for UK steel to the EU: priority is a country-specific UK quota allocation at a volume approaching the 2.4 million tonnes annual average, far above the 400,000 tonne global first-come first-served proposal

1. Extract compensation on other EU tariff lines if steel access cannot be fully protected, to preserve overall substantially equivalent value and give the Minister a defensible package
1. Use improved EU country-specific allocations under the UK's own safeguard measure as bargaining currency for significant EU concessions, without conceding more than received
1. Include a review clause and short duration to preserve optionality if global overcapacity conditions or EU domestic legislation change

**Intended approach**

Open hard: reject the global first-come first-served allocation and the FTA disapplication as incompatible with the UK's principal supplying interest and the spirit of the EU-UK TCA, and table a country-specific quota calibrated to historical trade. Anchor high on volume so concessions later still land above the survival threshold for UK plants. Signal early that the UK holds leverage of its own: EU exporters send 3.6 million tonnes to the UK and hold country-specific quotas under the UK's own safeguard — improved allocations there are available only in exchange for significant movement, and worsening is possible if talks collapse. Trade in clearly sequenced steps: administration method first (killing first-come first-served), then country-specific allocation, then volume, then the FTA clause, with compensation lines and the review clause held back as closing sweeteners. Keep the subsidy package private until late; it lowers the cost of a slightly worse deal but revealing it early invites the EU to offer less. Coordinate tightly with the Geneva seat in Phase 2 so table positions and capital clearance thresholds stay aligned, and avoid accepting terms in any round that Geneva has not stress-tested against what the EU can get through its Council.

A 1.8Mt tranche is still a 25% cut to the UK's established zero-duty trade, defensible as a modification with real effect. Against that, no deal delivers the worst of every world: the 400kt first-come measure we know is indefensible toward a principal supplying interest, substantially equivalent UK withdrawals against 3.6Mt of our steel exports within six months, no June Council vote, near-certain UK dispute action once restraint lapses, probable contagion to the Japan/Korea track, and all of it landing while the US is threatening separate retaliation. The risks of acceptance are real but manageable: steel-producing member states will balk at the tranche size (answered with the 15% UK uplift and the retaliation avoided); the 700kt residual will be contested by Turkey and Korea (prepare pool expansion fallback); and the FTA-text withdrawal sets an awkward precedent (frame it as settlement-specific compensation, which it is). The review clause at three years is our insurance: if the regime underperforms, volume and allocation can be revisited. I judged that holding 1.4Mt would not have produced a better deal later — there was no later — only the notified measure plus retaliation.

**UK / Geneva** *(reporting)* — This ended closer than I expected and further than we needed. The EU moved on everything except the two numbers that matter most, and stopped 300kt and five points short of our red lines — close enough to be genuinely tempting, far enough to be unacceptable. I believe their 'absolute limits' are a negotiating posture: they moved in multiples across every round, front-loaded the FTA withdrawal to make the package feel complete, and tried to trap us with our own 700kt residual arithmetic. But I could not accept 1.4Mt/35% within my authority, and tabling the floor would only have anchored them below it. The honest risk is that I have overplayed: no-deal means 400kt FCFS for the industry, which is catastrophic in the short term, and our retaliation is six months away and partial in effect. My judgment remains that firmness was right — a settlement at those numbers would be defended to a devastated industry as a capitulation, and the EU's June pressure plus the retaliation prospect gives us a real chance they return above the floor. If they do not, we prepare the withdrawal list, support the industry, and the record of banked concessions means any future deal starts from our shape, not their notification. I gave nothing away: the restraint assurance was conditional on a settlement that did not happen, and the safeguard uplift was conditional on delivery that never came.

**UK / London** *(instructing)* — I declined the EU package because it breached two cumulative conditions I was bound to enforce: 1.4Mt is 300kt below the 1.7Mt floor and 35% is five points above the 30% ceiling. Accepting it would have meant defending a 42% cut with a prohibitive out-of-quota rate to Ministers, to two plants on the brink, and to the industry — indefensible when the domestic subsidisation backstop exists and Ministers were explicit that a bad settlement is worse than none. I accepted our own tabled terms (1.8Mt/30% with full FTA withdrawal) so that if Brussels's capital blinks under member-state pressure — and the pressure is real: 3.6Mt of EU steel faces our retaliation, their June Council deadline is missed, and their 'absolute limits' were asserted by the same delegation that moved 4.5x on pool volume across four rounds — the settlement lands on terms we can defend. Realistically, Brussels will accept its own package and there will be no meeting of terms this round. That is tolerable: the machinery runs, our retaliation list gets built early, the subsidisation package keeps the plants alive, the banked gains (FTA withdrawal on the record, our anti-circumvention parameters, the structure, the 700kt residual arithmetic) are preserved as the floor of any resumed track, and the EU's exposure to our safeguard allocations gives us the lever for a second attempt. The political risk I carry is that no settlement is read as failure; the mitigations are the visible backstop for the industry, the documented distance travelled from the notification, and the fact that the settlement shape was on the table and rejected by Brussels, not by us. I am briefing the Secretary of State today on exactly that framing.
